# Regulatory evidence: what governs skincare ad claims in India

Collected 2026-09-05. This is the "policy & claims" dimension's source of truth. Where I could not get verbatim statute text, I say so.

## Instruments that apply

| Instrument | What it does | Relevance to Minimalist ads |
|---|---|---|
| **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940** + **Cosmetics Rules, 2020** | Defines "cosmetic" vs "drug". Rule 36 (Cosmetics Rules 2020): a cosmetic may not "purport or claim to convey a false or misleading idea" | A cosmetic that claims to *treat, cure or prevent* a disease is making a drug claim → product is misbranded / would need drug licensing |
| **Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954** | Prohibits ads claiming to cure/prevent listed conditions and any "magic" or miraculous effect. Cognizable offence. | "Cure acne", "permanent", "miracle", "guaranteed" language |
| **Schedule J, Drugs Rules 1945** | 54 conditions for which drug ads may not claim prevention/cure. Includes conditions adjacent to skincare: "premature ageing", "skin fairness"/complexion, hair loss (baldness), "rejuvenation" | Applies to *drugs*, not cosmetics (Vidhi analysis). But if an ad uses cure/treat language for these, it is making a drug claim and Schedule J becomes the standard it fails |
| **ASCI Code** (self-regulatory, Advertising Standards Council of India) | Chapter I clauses (verbatim, see below): 1.1 substantiation, 1.2 research source/date, 1.4 no misleading by implication/omission/exaggeration. Also guidelines on disclaimers, and on skin-colour discrimination in fairness ads | Meta/Google in India route complaints through ASCI. 98% of scrutinised beauty ads required modification (ASCI 2023-24 report) |
| **Consumer Protection Act, 2019** + **CCPA Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements, 2022** | Misleading ads are actionable; disclaimers cannot cure a false headline claim. CCPA example: "dermatologists recommend this product for all skin types" cannot be saved by "individual results may vary" in small font if no actual recommendation exists | "Dermatologist recommended/tested", "clinically proven" without evidence |

## ASCI Code Chapter I — verbatim clauses relied on

- **1.1** "Advertisements must be truthful. All descriptions, claims and comparisons which relate to matters of objectively ascertainable fact should be capable of substantiation."
- **1.2** "Where advertising claims are expressly stated to be based on or supported by independent research or assessment, its source and date should be indicated in the advertisement."
- **1.4** "Advertisements shall neither distort facts nor mislead the consumer by means of implications or omissions. Advertisements shall not contain statements or visual presentation which directly or by implication or by omission or by ambiguity or by exaggeration are likely to mislead the consumer about the product advertised or the advertiser or about any other product or advertiser."
- Chapter I preamble: advertisements "shall not be so framed as to abuse the trust of consumers or exploit their lack of experience or knowledge."

Source: ASCI Code text as reproduced at iprmentlaw.com/ASCI-Code.pdf and ascionline.in/the-asci-code. Clause 1.2 matters for Minimalist specifically: the brand cites study results ("50% after 28 days"); an ad that repeats the number without the study basis is technically short of 1.2.

## Claim categories and how they land

**Drug-like (highest risk — reclassifies the product):**
- cure / treat / heal / prevent + a condition (acne, eczema, psoriasis, dermatitis, infection, hair loss)
- "eliminates", "permanently", "gets rid of"
- Note: Minimalist's own pages say "Reduces Acne", "wards off acne", "Anti-acne" (tag). This is the industry's grey zone: *reducing the appearance of* acne is a cosmetic claim; *treating* acne is a drug claim. The pages stay on the cosmetic side by using "reduces", "helps", "fights breakouts".

**Unsubstantiated authority (high risk):**
- "clinically proven" / "dermatologist tested" / "scientifically proven" with no study, number, or population attached
- Minimalist's pages DO use "clinically proven" — but always bound to a measured result ("reduce number of blackheads by 50% after 28 days"). The rule is not "ban the phrase"; it is "the phrase must carry its evidence".

**Absolute / guarantee (high risk):**
- "100%", "guaranteed", "instant", "overnight", "no side effects", "safe for everyone", "suitable for all skin types"
- Minimalist pages specify age ("16+", "18+") and pregnancy status per product — they do not say "for everyone".

**Comparative / superiority (medium risk):**
- "best", "#1", "better than", "dermatologists' choice" — need substantiation under ASCI Chapter IV (fair competition)

**Fairness / skin-tone (medium-high, reputational + ASCI):**
- "fairness", "whitening", "lightening" of overall skin tone. ASCI guidelines prohibit depicting darker skin negatively. Minimalist uses "even skin tone", "reduce melanin concentration" (mechanism), "dark spots" — never "fair" or "white".

**Fear-based (brand-prohibited, not legally prohibited):**
- "chemical-free", "toxin-free", "no nasties", "harmful chemicals" — the founder explicitly names this as the misinformation the brand exists to counter. Legal under ASCI (arguably), but the single most off-brand thing an ad could say.

- ASCI clauses 1.3 and 1.5 (not needed for the rules; 1.1, 1.2, 1.4 obtained verbatim from a mirrored copy of the Code PDF, not from a primary ASCI download).
